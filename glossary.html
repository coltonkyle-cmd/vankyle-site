Glossary

Affordable housing terms

41 terms you'll run into, each in a sentence or two, with a link to the lesson that explains it.

AMI (area median income)
The middle household income for an area, published by HUD and adjusted for household size. Tax credit income limits are set as a percentage of it, usually 50% or 60%. Lesson: Who can live there, and how rent is set →
Applicable fraction
The share of a building that's affordable, measured by units or floor space, whichever is lower. Multiplied by eligible basis to get qualified basis. Lesson: Three numbers, one award →
Applicable percentage (credit rate)
The annual credit rate: at least 9% for new competitive deals and at least 4% for bond-financed deals. Lesson: 9% vs 4% deals →
Basis boost
Counting 130% of eligible basis for a building in a QCT, a DDA or one the state designates, which raises the credit amount. Lesson: The 30% basis boost →
Capital stack
All the sources of money that pay for a deal, layered from the first mortgage down to equity. Lesson: The capital stack →
Compliance period
The first 15 years of a tax credit property's life, when the IRS can recapture credits if the rules are broken. Lesson: The 30-year promise →
CRA (Community Reinvestment Act)
A federal law that encourages banks to invest in the communities they serve. Tax credit investments can count, which is why banks are big buyers. Lesson: Why $1 of credit sells for about 83 cents →
DDA (Difficult Development Area)
An area HUD designates where land, construction and utility costs are high relative to incomes. Deals there can get the 30% basis boost. Lesson: The 30% basis boost →
Deferred developer fee
The part of the developer fee left in the deal as a source of funds and paid later from cash flow. Lesson: The developer fee, and why part of it waits →
DSCR (debt service coverage ratio)
Net operating income divided by the annual mortgage payment. Lenders require a minimum, often 1.15 to 1.25, which limits how big the loan can be. Lesson: Why the bank lends so little →
Eligible basis
The costs of a building that can earn credits: mostly construction and related soft costs, not land. Lesson: What counts in eligible basis →
Equity (tax credit equity)
The cash investors pay into a deal in exchange for its tax credits and tax losses. Lesson: From award to cash →
Extended use
At least 15 more years of affordability after the compliance period, enforced by the state through a recorded agreement. Lesson: The 30-year promise →
Form 8609
The IRS form the state issues for each building once it's placed in service, confirming its credit amount. The owner needs it to claim credits. Lesson: How the equity actually gets paid in →
Form 8823
The form a state agency files with the IRS to report a property's noncompliance. Lesson: When a property breaks the rules →
General partner (GP)
The owner that runs the deal, usually a developer affiliate, holding a tiny ownership share. Lesson: Who actually owns a tax credit deal →
Gross rent
Rent plus a utility allowance for tenant-paid utilities. The tax credit cap applies to gross rent. Lesson: Why your rent is lower than the cap →
HOME
A HUD block grant program that states and cities use for affordable housing loans and grants. Lesson: The four most common soft-money sources →
Housing Choice Voucher (Section 8)
Rental assistance that helps a household pay rent at a private apartment. The renter usually pays about 30% of income. Lesson: They can't turn you down for having a voucher →
Income averaging
A set-aside option where unit income limits range from 20% to 80% of AMI but must average 60% or less. Lesson: Income averaging in four rules →
LIHTC
The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit, Section 42 of the tax code. The main federal tool for building affordable rental housing. Lesson: What LIHTC is →
Limited partner (LP)
The investor in a tax credit partnership, usually owning 99% or more to receive the credits. Lesson: Who actually owns a tax credit deal →
LURA
Land use restriction agreement: the recorded document that keeps a property affordable for its full term, whoever owns it. Lesson: The 30-year promise →
Market study
A third-party report, required before an award, showing there's demand for the apartments at the proposed rents. Lesson: The market study →
Minimum set-aside
The share of units a deal must keep affordable: 20/50, 40/60 or income averaging. Lesson: Who can live there, and how rent is set →
MTSP income limits
The income limits HUD publishes each year for tax credit and bond-financed properties. Lesson: When rents can go up →
NOI (net operating income)
Rental income minus vacancy and operating costs, before the mortgage payment. Lesson: Why the bank lends so little →
Placed in service
When a building is ready for residents. Starts the credit and compliance clocks. Lesson: You won. The clock started. →
Private activity bonds
Tax-exempt bonds issued for private projects like affordable housing. Using enough of them unlocks 4% credits. Lesson: The cap behind every 4% deal →
QAP (Qualified Allocation Plan)
Each state's rulebook and scoring system for awarding tax credits. Lesson: What every state has to reward →
QCT (Qualified Census Tract)
A lower-income census tract designated by HUD. Deals there can get the 30% basis boost. Lesson: The 30% basis boost →
Qualified basis
Eligible basis times the applicable fraction. Multiplied by the credit rate to get the annual credit. Lesson: Three numbers, one award →
Qualified contract
A process that can let an owner end the affordability period after year 14 if the state can't find a buyer at a formula price. Many states require owners to waive it. Lesson: Year 15: how the investor exits →
RAD
The Rental Assistance Demonstration: converts public housing to long-term Section 8 contracts so it can be financed and rebuilt. Lesson: How old public housing gets rebuilt (RAD) →
Recapture
The IRS taking back part of the credits already claimed, with interest, if a building's qualified basis drops during the compliance period. Lesson: When a property breaks the rules →
Right of first refusal (ROFR)
A right for a nonprofit, tenant group or agency to buy a property after year 15 at a minimum price. Lesson: Year 15: how the investor exits →
Soft money
Below-market or forgivable loans and grants that fill financing gaps. Lesson: The four most common soft-money sources →
Syndicator
A firm that pools investor money into funds and invests it in tax credit deals. Lesson: Why $1 of credit sells for about 83 cents →
Utility allowance
An estimate of tenant-paid utility costs, subtracted from the gross rent cap to get the maximum rent. Lesson: Why your rent is lower than the cap →
Volume cap
Each state's annual limit on private activity bonds. 4% deals compete for it. Lesson: The cap behind every 4% deal →
Year 15
The end of the compliance period, when investors usually exit and ownership often changes hands. Lesson: Year 15: how the investor exits →
