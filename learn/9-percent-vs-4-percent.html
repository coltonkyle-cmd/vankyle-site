9% vs 4% deals

There are two flavors of credit.

The course / Module 1: LIHTC 101 / Lesson 1.5

Last reviewed November 2026

There are two flavors of credit. The names come from roughly how much of a building's eligible cost the credits are worth each year.

  • 9% credits are designed to be worth about 70% of eligible costs in present value. They're scarce, so states award them competitively through the QAP. The 9% rate is now a fixed minimum for new construction and rehab.
  • 4% credits are designed to be worth about 30%. They don't come out of the state's competitive budget. Instead, a project gets them automatically if enough of it is financed with tax-exempt private activity bonds. The 4% rate has been a fixed minimum since 2021.

The trade-off: 9% deals bring much more equity but are hard to win. 4% deals are easier to get but leave a bigger gap that soft money, a bigger loan or a deferred fee has to fill. Either way, credits are claimed over 10 years.

Key points
  • 9%: about 70% of costs in value, competitive.
  • 4%: about 30%, comes with tax-exempt bonds.
  • 4% deals need more money from other sources.
  • Both are claimed over 10 years.

Why the rates are "at least"

The rates used to float monthly, set by the Treasury to hit the 70% and 30% values. Congress later locked in minimums: 9% for new construction and substantial rehab that isn't federally subsidized, and 4% for buildings that received allocations, or were financed by bonds issued, after 2020. Buying an existing building earns the 4% rate. Today both work as fixed rates.

See the difference

Put the same building through the capital stack calculator and flip between 9% and 4%. The 4% version brings in much less equity, so the gap gets much bigger.

Example, made-up numbers: $17M of eligible basis. At 9%, that's $1.53M of credit a year; at 83 cents, about $12.7M of equity. At 4%, it's $680K a year, or about $5.6M of equity. Same building, $7M less.

So why would anyone do a 4% deal?

  • There's no annual competition to win, so it can move on the developer's timeline.
  • Bigger projects that would blow past a state's per-deal 9% limit can still work.
  • The bonds can carry an attractive loan, and some states pair 4% deals with their own soft money or state credits.

Some deals even combine the two on separate buildings, where the state allows it.
