The 30% basis boost

Two identical buildings can get very different awards.

The course / Module 2: Sizing the credit / Lesson 2.2

Last reviewed November 2026

Two identical buildings can get very different awards. The difference is the address.

In certain areas, the tax code lets a deal count 130% of its eligible basis (for an existing building, only the rehab costs get the boost, not the purchase price):

  • Qualified Census Tracts (QCTs): tracts where at least half of households earn under 60% of area median income, or the poverty rate is 25% or more.
  • Difficult Development Areas (DDAs): places where land, construction and utility costs are high compared to local incomes.
  • State-designated buildings: a state agency can give the boost to a 9% building that needs it to be financially feasible. This one isn't available to bond-financed 4% deals.
Example, made-up numbers: $10M of basis becomes $13M. At 9%, that's $1.17M of credit a year instead of $900K, about $2.7M more over 10 years.

HUD redraws the QCT and DDA maps every year. The 2027 lists were announced in October 2026. A boost for all rural and tribal areas was proposed in 2025 but didn't make it into law.

Key points
  • Count 130% of eligible basis.
  • QCTs: lower-income tracts.
  • DDAs: high-cost areas.
  • Or the state designates the building (9% only).
  • The map changes every year.

The boost in dollars

Example, made-up numbersNo boostWith the 30% boost
Eligible basis$12,000,000$15,600,000
Annual credit (100% affordable, 9%)$1,080,000$1,404,000
Credits over 10 years$10,800,000$14,040,000
Equity at 83 cents$8,964,000$11,653,200

Same building, same costs: about $2.7 million more equity. HUD designates an area as a QCT or a DDA, not both, and the boost is capped at 130% either way.

How the maps are drawn

  • QCTs are census tracts. HUD uses Census income and poverty data, and the tracts it picks can't hold more than 20% of a metro area's population.
  • DDAs compare costs to incomes, using HUD's Fair Market Rents and income limits. In metro areas, HUD has drawn DDAs by ZIP code since 2016 (called Small Area DDAs). Outside metro areas, it uses whole counties. DDAs also can't cover more than 20% of the population.

What if the map changes?

Because the lists change every year, HUD's notices include a grace period. If a complete application is filed while the site is on the list, and before the next year’s lists take effect, the deal generally keeps the boost as long as the credits are awarded (or, for a 4% deal, the bonds are issued or the building is placed in service) within 730 days of that application, under HUD’s 2027 notice. Check the exact rule in the current HUD notice for any real deal.

The state-designated boost

Since 2008, a state can give the boost to a 9% building outside a QCT or DDA if the state decides the building needs it to be financially feasible. Each state sets its own rules in its plan (the QAP). Some use it for rural deals, some for high-opportunity areas, and some barely use it. Bond-financed 4% deals can't use this route; they need a QCT or DDA.

Check an address

HUD publishes the QCT and DDA lists and an online map where you can look up any address. Start at HUD User's QCT and DDA page.
