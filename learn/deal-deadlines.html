Module 6 · Lesson 6.1

You won. The clock started.

Winning tax credits feels like the finish line.

The course / Module 6: The long game / Lesson 6.1

Last reviewed November 2026

Winning tax credits feels like the finish line. It's really the starting gun.

  • Before the award: a market study, paid for by the developer, done by an independent firm the state approves.
  • Within 12 months: if the building won't be finished in the award year, the owner's basis in the project has to be more than 10% of its expected total within a year of the allocation.
  • By the end of year 2: the building has to be placed in service by the end of the second calendar year after the award year.
Example, made-up numbers: award in June 2027. More than 10% of expected basis in place by June 2028. Placed in service by December 31, 2029.

The 10% test and the placed-in-service deadline apply to 9% allocations. 4% bond deals still need the market study, but they don't get an allocation from the state's credit ceiling, so the rest of their clock runs on bond and issuer timelines. Miss a deadline and the allocation fails; the credits go back to the state to hand out again. Closing the land, the loans and the investor, permits, and construction all have to fit inside that window.

Key points
  • Market study before the award.
  • More than 10% of basis within 12 months.
  • Placed in service by the end of year 2.
  • Miss it and the credits go back.

The rules in plain English

  • Market study first. Before the state allocates credits, a comprehensive market study of local housing need has to be done by a disinterested party the agency approves, at the developer's expense. See the market study lesson.
  • The 10% test. A building placed in service in the award year doesn't need it. Every other 9% award does. One year after the allocation date, the owner's basis in the project has to be more than 10% of the basis it reasonably expects to have when the building is done. The state has to verify it, usually with a certification from the owner or from a CPA or attorney.
  • Placed in service by the end of year 2. Counted in calendar years, not months: an award any time in 2027 means a December 31, 2029 deadline.
  • After that, a new clock. The 10-year credit period starts the year the building is placed in service, or the next year if the owner elects. The minimum set-aside has to be met by the end of the first year of the credit period, and the 15-year compliance period starts with that first credit year.

Example: does the deal pass the 10% test?

Example, made-up numbers: the state signs a carryover allocation on October 15, 2027. The project's expected basis, land plus everything depreciable, is $20,000,000. By October 15, 2028 the owner needs more than $2,000,000 of basis.
Land closed: $1,500,000. Architecture, engineering and permits: $400,000. That's $1,900,000, short. Add $200,000 of early site work and fees paid, and the total is $2,100,000, which passes.

Land counts toward this test, even though land never counts in eligible basis and never earns credits. That's one reason many deals close on the land early.

Why the award date matters

Same deadline, almost a year less time. A deal awarded late in the year has to close and start building fast.

How it works in practice

  1. Award and carryover. The state reserves the credits, then signs a carryover allocation agreement, usually by the end of the award year.
  2. Closing. Land, construction loan, permanent loan commitment, investor and any soft money all close, often 6 to 12 months after the award.
  3. Construction. Often 12 to 24 months, depending on size and type.
  4. Placed in service. For new construction, generally when the first unit is certified as ready to live in.
  5. Cost certification and Form 8609. An accountant certifies the final costs, and the state issues IRS Form 8609, which the owner needs to claim the credits.

4% bond deals run on a different clock

A 4% deal gets its credits from tax-exempt bonds instead of the state's 9% ceiling, so there's no carryover allocation and no 10% test. Its pressure points are the bond side: winning a share of the state's private activity bond cap, issuing the bonds before that award expires under state rules, and financing enough of the building with them. The bond test is 50% of the building and land, or 25% if bonds from an issue dated after 2025 finance at least 5% of the building and land. See the bond cap lesson.

Common mistakes

  • "12 months from the end of the year." No. The 10% test runs from the allocation date.
  • "Land doesn't count." It does for the 10% test, even though it doesn't for eligible basis.
  • "The 15 years start at the award." No. The compliance period starts with the first year of the credit period, after the building is placed in service.
  • "A missed deadline just means a late fee." A missed federal deadline means the allocation fails and the credits go back to the state. Some states have their own deadlines and return-and-reapply policies, but outside IRS disaster relief they can't extend the federal deadlines.
