The team it takes to build one deal

One tax credit deal takes a lot of people.

The course / Module 3: Winning the award / Lesson 3.2

Last reviewed November 2026

One tax credit deal takes a lot of people. The core seats:

  • Developer: finds the site, runs the application, guarantees the deal.
  • Architect and engineers: design to code, to the QAP and to accessibility rules.
  • General contractor: prices and builds it.
  • Syndicator or investor: puts in equity in exchange for the credits.
  • Lenders: a construction loan and a permanent loan, sometimes the same bank.
  • Attorneys: usually several, for the developer, investor, lender and bonds.
  • Market analyst: proves there's demand.
  • Property manager: leases it up and keeps it in compliance for decades.

Add a CPA for the cost certification, often a tax credit consultant, and on public housing deals, the housing authority. The investor, the lenders and the state all have to approve before closing.

Key points
  • At least eight core seats.
  • Several attorneys, not one.
  • The property manager matters for 30+ years.
  • Investor, lenders and the state all approve before closing.

Who's working when

StageWho's busy

Award to closingInvestor or syndicator, lenders, attorneys for every party, contractor finalizing price, title company, appraiser
ConstructionContractor, architect on site visits, the lender's and investor's construction inspectors
Lease-upProperty manager and its compliance staff

The next 15 to 30+ yearsProperty manager, the investor's asset manager, the state's compliance monitors

The third-party reports

Before an application or a closing, outside firms produce a stack of reports. Common ones:

  • Phase I environmental site assessment: checks for contamination.
  • Market study: proves demand at the planned rents.
  • Appraisal: values the land and the finished property.
  • Survey and soils report: boundaries, easements and what the ground can hold.
  • Capital needs assessment: for rehab deals, what needs fixing and when.
  • Federal environmental review: required when HUD money, such as HOME funds or project-based vouchers, is in the deal.

Who guarantees what

The investor is putting in millions and wants someone on the hook if things go wrong. That's usually the developer and its parent company. Common guarantees to the investor:

  • Construction completion: the building gets finished, even if it costs more.
  • Operating deficits: the developer covers shortfalls in the early years, usually up to a cap and for a set period.
  • Credit delivery: if fewer credits come through than promised, the investor gets money back.

That's why investors look hard at a developer's net worth and cash, not just its track record.

Who pays before the award

Example, made-up numbers: a developer spends $250,000 on site control, reports, design and fees to put in an application. If the deal wins, most of that gets paid back from the development budget at closing. If it loses, the developer usually eats it, or tries again next round.
