Why your rent is lower than the cap

The rent cap on a tax credit apartment is a gross rent cap.

The course / Module 5: Living there / Lesson 5.2

Last reviewed November 2026

The rent cap on a tax credit apartment is a gross rent cap. It includes an allowance for any utilities you pay yourself, like electricity or gas. (Phone, cable and internet don't count.)

Example, made-up numbers: if the maximum gross rent is $1,200 and the utility allowance is $150, the most you can be charged in rent is $1,050.

Where the allowance comes from depends on the building. Properties with Rural Development or HUD project-based assistance use those agencies' figures. Others can use the local housing authority's schedule, or an estimate from the utility company, the state agency, HUD's utility model or an energy consumption model.

Allowances are reviewed at least once a year. A change usually takes effect after a 90-day notice period.

Key points
  • Rent cap includes utilities you pay.
  • Allowance = cap minus what you can be charged.
  • Source depends on the building.
  • Reviewed at least yearly.

Example: same building, different rents

Three two-bedroom apartments in one building, all with a $1,200 maximum gross rent (made-up numbers):

Who pays which utilitiesUtility allowanceMost you can be charged
Owner pays everything$0$1,200
You pay electric only$90$1,110
You pay electric, gas and water$180$1,020

The more utilities you pay yourself, the lower the rent cap on what you pay the landlord.

Look up the maximum gross rents by county

Subtract your building's utility allowance from the gross rent for your unit's income level and size. That's roughly the most the rent can be. The property manager has the exact numbers.

Which allowance applies

  • Rural Development buildings, or any building where a tenant gets Rural Development rental assistance: the Rural Development allowance.
  • HUD-regulated buildings, where HUD sets the rents and allowances: HUD's allowance.
  • Everyone else: units where the renter gets HUD rental assistance, such as a voucher, use the local public housing authority's allowance. For other units, the housing authority's allowance is the default, and the owner can instead use a written estimate from the utility company, an estimate from the state agency if it offers one, HUD's Utility Schedule Model, or an energy consumption model done by a licensed engineer or other qualified professional who isn't related to the owner.

How changes work

  • The owner has to review the allowance at least once each calendar year.
  • When an allowance changes, the new one has to be used for rents due 90 days after the change. If it goes up, the most you can be charged goes down.
  • Most owner-chosen estimates have to go to the state agency and be made available to tenants before that 90-day period starts.

Submeters

Some buildings buy utilities in bulk and bill each apartment through its own meter. If the bill reflects your actual use and the rate is no higher than the owner pays, it's treated as a utility you pay yourself, so it gets an allowance too. Any billing fee has to be figured the same way for every unit, and it's capped, generally at $5 a month unless a higher limit applies.

Common myths

  • "The rent cap is what I'll pay." The cap is gross rent: rent plus the allowance for utilities you pay.
  • "My rent should change with my utility bill." No. The allowance is an estimate for the building, not your actual bill.
  • "Internet counts as a utility." No. Phone, cable TV and internet don't count.

Questions you can ask the manager

  • Which utilities do I pay, and what's the allowance for my unit size?
  • Where does the allowance come from, and when was it last updated?
  • What's the maximum gross rent for my unit's income level?
