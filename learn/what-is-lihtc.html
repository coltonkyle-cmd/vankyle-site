What LIHTC is

The course / Module 1: LIHTC 101 / Lesson 1.1

The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit, or LIHTC, is a federal tax credit created by the Tax Reform Act of 1986. It lives in Section 42 of the tax code, which is why you'll hear people call these "Section 42 properties."

Every year, each state gets a budget of credits based on its population. The state's housing finance agency hands them out to apartment projects through a competition, using a scoring plan called the Qualified Allocation Plan (QAP).

The developer who wins doesn't keep the credits. An investor, usually a bank, puts cash into the project in exchange for them. That cash is equity. It means the building needs less debt, and less debt is what lets the rents stay low.

Example, made-up numbers: a project receives $1,000,000 of credits a year for 10 years, or $10 million total. If an investor pays 83 cents per dollar of credit, that's $8.3 million of equity for the building.
Key points
  • A federal tax credit, Section 42 of the tax code.
  • States award it through a competition.
  • Investors buy the credits; the cash builds the apartments.
  • Less debt means lower rents.

Where the credits come from

Congress sets the formula and the IRS adjusts it for inflation each year. For 2027, each state gets the greater of $3.53 per resident or $4,082,400. That pot covers the competitive 9% credits. The 4% credits work differently: they aren't capped by that formula, but they need tax-exempt bonds, and bonds are capped by a separate limit. See your state's credit budget and the bond cap.

Why a tax credit instead of a grant

The government could just write checks to build apartments. The tax credit does something different: it brings in private investors whose money is at risk for 15 years. If the property is badly run or breaks the rules, the investor can lose credits. That gives the investor a reason to watch the deal closely, which is a big part of why the program has lasted since 1986.

It also means the property is privately owned and privately managed. The government sets the rules and checks compliance, but it doesn't run the building.

How big it is

Since 1986, the program has helped finance about 4 million affordable homes, built or preserved. It's the largest source of funding for new affordable rental housing in the country.

Who's involved

  • The state housing finance agency writes the rules and awards the credits. See what QAPs reward.
  • The developer finds the site, applies, builds and usually stays on as the general partner. See the deal team.
  • The investor buys the credits, usually through a syndicator. See who owns the deal.
  • The residents have to income-qualify. See who can live there.

Common questions

Is it a tax break for the developer? Not really. The developer sells the credits. The tax benefit goes to the investor, and the cash goes into the building.

Do residents get a tax credit? No. Residents get a lower, capped rent.

Is it the same as Section 8? No. Section 8 vouchers help a renter pay rent. The tax credit helps pay to build the apartment. A renter can use a voucher at a tax credit property, though. See the voucher rule.
