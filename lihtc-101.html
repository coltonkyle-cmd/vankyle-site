1.1 What LIHTC is

The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit, or LIHTC, is a federal tax credit created by the Tax Reform Act of 1986. It lives in Section 42 of the tax code, which is why you'll hear people call these "Section 42 properties."

Every year, each state gets a budget of credits based on its population. The state's housing finance agency hands them out to apartment projects through a competition, using a scoring plan called the Qualified Allocation Plan (QAP).

The developer who wins doesn't keep the credits. An investor, usually a bank, puts cash into the project in exchange for them. That cash is equity. It means the building needs less debt, and less debt is what lets the rents stay low.

Example, made-up numbers: a project receives $1,000,000 of credits a year for 10 years, or $10 million total. If an investor pays 83 cents per dollar of credit, that's $8.3 million of equity for the building.
Key points
  • A federal tax credit, Section 42 of the tax code.
  • States award it through a competition.
  • Investors buy the credits; the cash builds the apartments.
  • Less debt means lower rents.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42.
  • IRS, Low-Income Housing Credit overview.

1.2 It's not public housing

People mix these up all the time. Public housing is owned and run by a local housing authority, and residents usually pay about 30% of their own income in rent.

Most LIHTC properties are privately owned and privately managed. The rent is capped by the unit, based on the area's income limits, not by each resident's paycheck. A family earning less than the limit pays the same capped rent as a family right at the limit, unless they also have a voucher.

The two programs do overlap. When an old public housing site gets rebuilt, it's often financed with tax credits, and some units may keep deeper rental assistance. That's covered in the RAD lesson.

Key points
  • Public housing: owned by a housing authority; rent based on the resident's income.
  • LIHTC: usually private owners; rent capped by the unit's income limit.
  • They can be combined, especially in public housing rebuilds.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(g) (rent restrictions).
  • HUD, Public Housing Program overview.

1.3 The 30-year promise

A tax credit deal isn't a 10-year bet. There are three clocks:

  • Years 1–10: the credit period. Investors claim the credits a little at a time.
  • Years 1–15: the compliance period. Incomes get checked and rents stay capped. If the property breaks the rules, the IRS can take credits back.
  • Years 16–30+: extended use. At least 15 more years of affordability, recorded against the property and enforced by the state.

That's at least 30 years, and plenty of states ask for longer. California's agreements, for example, run 55 years. Ownership often changes hands around year 15, but the rent rules stay with the property. More on that in the Year 15 lesson.

Key points
  • 10 years of credits.
  • 15-year compliance period, enforced by the IRS.
  • At least 15 more years of extended use, enforced by the state.
  • Many states require longer.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(f)(1), § 42(i)(1), § 42(h)(6), § 42(j).
  • California Tax Credit Allocation Committee regulations (55-year term).

1.4 Who can live there, and how rent is set

Every deal picks one of three minimum set-asides:

  • 20/50: at least 20% of units for households earning 50% or less of area median income (AMI).
  • 40/60: at least 40% of units for households at 60% of AMI or less.
  • Income averaging: at least 40% of units, with income limits that average 60% of AMI or less and no unit above 80%. See the income averaging lesson.

In practice, most deals make nearly every unit affordable, because credits are only earned on the affordable units.

Rent is capped too. Gross rent, meaning rent plus a utility allowance, can't be more than 30% of the income limit for that unit. The limit is figured using 1.5 people per bedroom, so a two-bedroom is priced off a three-person household.

One rule surprises people: a household made up entirely of full-time students generally doesn't qualify, with some exceptions such as single parents and students who receive certain public assistance.

Example, made-up numbers: if the 60% AMI income limit for a three-person household is $48,000, the maximum gross rent for a two-bedroom at 60% is 30% × $48,000 ÷ 12 = $1,200 a month, including the utility allowance.
Key points
  • Three set-aside options: 20/50, 40/60, income averaging.
  • Gross rent capped at 30% of the unit's income limit.
  • Rent is priced off 1.5 people per bedroom.
  • All-student households generally don't qualify.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(g)(1) (set-asides), § 42(g)(2) (rent limits, imputed household size), § 42(i)(3)(D) (students).
  • HUD, Multifamily Tax Subsidy Project (MTSP) income limits.

1.5 9% vs 4% deals

There are two flavors of credit. The names come from roughly how much of a building's eligible cost the credits are worth each year.

  • 9% credits are designed to be worth about 70% of eligible costs in present value. They're scarce, so states award them competitively through the QAP. The 9% rate is now a fixed minimum for new buildings.
  • 4% credits are designed to be worth about 30%. They don't come out of the state's competitive budget. Instead, a project gets them automatically if enough of it is financed with tax-exempt private activity bonds. The 4% rate has been a fixed minimum since 2021.

The trade-off: 9% deals bring much more equity but are hard to win. 4% deals are easier to get but leave a bigger gap that soft money, a bigger loan or a deferred fee has to fill. Either way, credits are claimed over 10 years.

Key points
  • 9%: about 70% of costs in value, competitive.
  • 4%: about 30%, comes with tax-exempt bonds.
  • 4% deals need more money from other sources.
  • Both are claimed over 10 years.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(b) (applicable percentages and minimums), § 42(h)(4) (bond-financed buildings).
  • Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021 (4% minimum).

1.6 The capital stack

No affordable deal is paid for by one source. It's a stack, and every piece comes with strings.

  • Tax credit equity. Usually the biggest piece. No monthly payment, but strict rules for decades.
  • A first mortgage. A regular loan. Rents are capped, so the loan has to stay small enough for the property to pay it.
  • Soft loans and grants. State, city or Federal Home Loan Bank money. Cheap or forgiven, but competitive and slow.
  • Deferred developer fee. The developer leaves part of their fee in the deal and gets paid later from cash flow. It closes the last gap.
Example, made-up numbers: a $20 million deal paid for with $13M of tax credit equity (65%), a $4M first mortgage (20%), $2M of soft money (10%) and a $1M deferred developer fee (5%).

Module 4, Paying for it, takes each piece apart.

Key points
  • Every deal is layered.
  • Equity is the biggest piece.
  • The mortgage is limited by capped rents.
  • Soft money and deferred fee fill the gap.
Sources
  • Example numbers are made up for illustration.

1.7 Why $1 of credit sells for about 83 cents

Developers don't get a dollar for every dollar of credit. In the second quarter of 2026, investors paid about 82.7 cents on average, down from about 85 cents a year earlier.

Why the discount:

  • Investors wait. The credits come in over 10 years. A dollar in year 10 is worth less than a dollar today.
  • Investors take risk. If the property breaks the rules during the 15-year compliance period, the IRS can take credits back.
  • Investors want a return. Paying less than $1 now is how they earn one.

Some investors pay more. Banks get Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) credit for investing in certain markets, so pricing tends to be stronger where banks want CRA credit. And when supply jumped in 2026, with 12% more 9% credits, prices slipped.

Key points
  • Pricing is cents per dollar of credit.
  • Time, risk and return explain the discount.
  • CRA demand pushes prices up in some markets.
  • About 83 cents in mid-2026.
Sources
  • Affordable Housing Finance, 2026 midyear LIHTC investor survey (Q2 2026 average of 82.7 cents).
  • CohnReznick Tax Credit Monitor (Feb–May 2026 median of about $0.835).
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(f), § 42(j).

1.8 What changed in 2026

The tax law signed July 4, 2025 (Public Law 119-21) made two big changes for affordable housing, both starting in 2026:

  • 12% more 9% credits, permanently. Each state's annual credit budget went up 12%. That's why the 2026 amount was $3.416 per resident.
  • An easier bond test. 4% deals used to need tax-exempt bonds covering 50% of the building and land cost. For bonds issued after 2025, it's 25%. That stretches each state's bond cap across more projects.

What didn't make it: a 30% basis boost for rural and tribal areas was in the House version but dropped from the final law.

The catch: more credits, same pool of investors. Pricing softened, as covered in the lesson above.

Key points
  • Signed July 4, 2025.
  • 9% credits up 12%, permanently.
  • 4% bond test cut from 50% to 25%.
  • Rural and tribal basis boost was not included.
Sources
  • Public Law 119-21 (2025).
  • IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 (2026 per-capita amount).
  • EY Tax News, July 10, 2025; Moss Adams, 2025 (rural boost dropped).
