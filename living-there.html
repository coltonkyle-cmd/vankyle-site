5.1 They can't turn you down for having a voucher

Plenty of landlords turn down Section 8 vouchers. Tax credit properties can't do that just because you have one.

Every tax credit property signs a long-term agreement with the state. Federal law requires it to prohibit refusing a renter because they hold a Section 8 voucher. It lasts as long as the affordability rules do: at least 30 years.

It's not a free pass. You still have to income-qualify and meet the same screening rules as every other applicant.

The two programs fit together: the tax credit caps the rent on the apartment, and the voucher helps the renter pay it. To find properties near you, search for HUD's LIHTC database.

Key points
  • It's in every long-term agreement.
  • No refusing just for a voucher.
  • You still have to income-qualify.
  • Lasts 30+ years.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(h)(6)(B)(iv).
  • HUD LIHTC Database (huduser.gov).
  • State and local source-of-income laws vary; not legal advice.

5.2 Why your rent is lower than the cap

The rent cap on a tax credit apartment is a gross rent cap. It includes an allowance for any utilities you pay yourself, like electricity or gas. (Phone, cable and internet don't count.)

Example, made-up numbers: if the maximum gross rent is $1,200 and the utility allowance is $150, the most you can be charged in rent is $1,050.

Where the allowance comes from depends on the building. Properties with Rural Development or HUD project-based assistance use those agencies' figures. Others can use the local housing authority's schedule, or an estimate from the utility company, the state agency, HUD's utility model or an energy consumption model.

Allowances are reviewed at least once a year. A change usually takes effect after a 90-day notice period.

Key points
  • Rent cap includes utilities you pay.
  • Allowance = cap minus what you can be charged.
  • Source depends on the building.
  • Reviewed at least yearly.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(g)(2)(B)(ii).
  • Treas. Reg. § 1.42-10.

5.3 Income averaging in four rules

Income averaging, added to the law in 2018, lets one building serve a wider range of incomes.

  1. At least 40% of units must be restricted.
  2. Each restricted unit gets a designated income limit in 10% steps, from 20% to 80% of AMI.
  3. The designations must average 60% of AMI or less.
  4. Each unit's rent is capped based on its own designated limit.
Example, made-up numbers: a 100-unit building designates 20 units at 30% AMI, 40 at 60% and 40 at 80%. The average is 62%, which fails. Move 10 of the 80% units down to 60%, and the average is exactly 60%, which works.

The appeal: deeply affordable units for very low-income families and workforce units in the same building, with the higher rents helping carry the lower ones.

Key points
  • 40% minimum.
  • Limits from 20% to 80% in 10% steps.
  • Average must be 60% or less.
  • Rent follows each unit's limit.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(g)(1)(C) (Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018).
  • Treas. Reg. § 1.42-19.

5.4 What happens if you get a raise

A common worry: if I earn more, does my rent go up or do I have to move? Usually, neither.

You have to qualify when you move in. After that, a raise doesn't kick you out. Your rent stays capped by the unit's limit, not your paycheck.

The one rule that kicks in is the 140% rule. If your household income rises above 140% of the current income limit, your unit still counts as affordable. But the owner must rent the next available unit of comparable or smaller size to a qualified household.

In buildings where every unit is affordable, federal law doesn't require yearly income checks after move-in, though many states still require them.

Key points
  • Qualify at move-in.
  • A raise doesn't change your rent cap.
  • Over 140%: the next available unit goes to a qualified renter.
  • Some states still require yearly income checks.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(g)(2)(D).
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(g)(8)(B) (recertification for 100% low-income buildings).
  • Not legal advice; property and state rules vary.

5.5 When rents can go up

Yes, rents at tax credit properties can go up. Here's how.

Every year, HUD publishes new income limits for tax credit properties. For fiscal 2026 they took effect May 1, 2026, and properties had a 45-day grace period to start using them. When the limits rise, the maximum rents rise too.

There are guardrails. HUD capped the increase at 10% for fiscal 2026, and the average change across areas was about 3.4%. A federal hold-harmless rule keeps a project's limits from falling below the highest level it has reached.

Owners aren't required to charge the maximum. Lease terms, notice rules and other funding sources can also limit increases. The utility allowance can change too, which moves the rent in the other direction.

Key points
  • Limits are updated every year by HUD.
  • Higher limits mean higher maximum rents.
  • FY2026 increases were capped at 10%.
  • Owners don't have to charge the max.
Sources
  • HUD, FY2026 Income Limits and MTSP Income Limits (huduser.gov).
  • National Law Review, "HUD Releases 2026 Income Limits" (2026) (10% cap, 3.4% average).
  • Minnesota Housing, FY2026 limits bulletin (45-day grace period).
  • Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008, § 3009 (hold harmless).

5.6 Senior vs. family deals

Many tax credit properties are age-restricted. Under fair housing law, there are two ways to do it:

  • 62 and older: every resident is 62+.
  • 55 and older: at least 80% of occupied units have at least one resident who's 55+, and the property follows policies showing it's meant for older adults.

They pencil differently than family deals. Senior deals are mostly one-bedrooms, so rent per unit is lower. They usually need elevators, more common space and more accessible units. Some states reserve credits or points specifically for elderly housing.

Family deals have more two- and three-bedroom units and higher rents per unit, but more wear, more turnover and different amenities, like playgrounds.

Key points
  • Two legal options: 62+ or 55+.
  • Senior deals skew to one-bedrooms.
  • Elevators and common space add cost.
  • Some QAPs set aside credits for seniors.
Sources
  • Fair Housing Act, 42 U.S.C. § 3607(b), as amended by the Housing for Older Persons Act of 1995.
  • 24 CFR Part 100, Subpart E.

5.7 The accessibility rules every new deal meets

Several layers of law apply, and a tax credit deal usually triggers more than one:

  • Fair Housing Act: for buildings with 4 or more units first occupied after March 13, 1991, every unit in an elevator building and every ground-floor unit otherwise must meet seven basic design requirements, like accessible routes, wider doors and reinforced bathroom walls.
  • Section 504: if the deal has federal financial assistance, like HOME or CDBG, at least 5% of units must be fully accessible for people with mobility disabilities and another 2% for people with hearing or vision disabilities.
  • ADA Title III: covers the leasing office and other areas open to the public.

On top of that, many state QAPs require or reward more accessible units. And for tax credit properties, a final fair housing discrimination finding is treated as noncompliance.

Key points
  • Fair Housing Act design rules apply to most new apartments.
  • Section 504 adds 5% mobility and 2% sensory units with federal funds.
  • ADA covers public areas.
  • States often require more.
Sources
  • 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(C); 24 CFR § 100.205.
  • 24 CFR § 8.22 (Section 504).
  • 28 CFR Part 36 (ADA Title III).
  • Treas. Reg. § 1.42-9 (general public use).

5.8 What supportive housing is

Supportive housing is affordable housing paired with services, like case management, health care coordination or job help, for people who need them to stay housed. That often means people leaving homelessness, people with disabilities, or veterans.

The housing is usually financed with tax credits like any other deal. What's different:

  • Rental assistance is usually needed so residents with very low or no income can pay rent.
  • Services are funded separately, by sources like Medicaid, HUD homeless programs or state funds.
  • Service fees paid by a government program aren't counted as rent under the tax credit rules.

Many QAPs give points for supportive housing units. The hard part is that all three pieces, building, rental assistance and services, have to be lined up for decades.

Key points
  • Housing plus services.
  • Usually needs rental assistance.
  • Services are funded separately.
  • All three pieces have to last.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(g)(2)(B)(iii) (supportive service fees); § 42(i)(3)(B)(iii) (transitional housing for the homeless).
