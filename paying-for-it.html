4.1 Who actually owns a tax credit deal

Developers win the credits. Then almost all of them go to someone else.

Each property is usually owned by its own partnership or LLC, set up just for that deal. The investor comes in as the limited partner and typically owns 99% to 99.99% of it. That's how it receives the credits and tax losses.

An affiliate of the developer is usually the general partner. It owns a tiny share, as little as 0.01%, but it runs the deal and carries the guarantees.

So how do developers get paid? Mostly through the developer fee, plus a negotiated share of cash flow and the eventual sale. As limited partners, investors generally can't lose more than they put in.

Key points
  • One partnership per deal.
  • Investor: 99% to 99.99%.
  • Developer side: a sliver, but runs it.
  • Developers earn fees.
Sources
  • Kutak Rock, "Introduction to Tax Credits" (CDFA).
  • ABA Journal of Affordable Housing & Community Development Law.
  • General description only; structures vary.

4.2 The six buckets in every development budget

The capital stack is where the money comes from. The development budget is where it goes. Almost every budget sorts into six buckets:

  1. Acquisition: land, plus any existing buildings.
  2. Hard costs: construction, site work and a contingency.
  3. Soft costs: architecture, engineering, legal, the market study, permits, insurance, application fees.
  4. Financing costs: construction interest and loan and bond fees.
  5. Developer fee: capped by the state.
  6. Reserves: operating, replacement and lease-up reserves the investor and lender require.
Example, made-up numbers: a $20M budget might be $1M acquisition, $14M hard costs, $1.5M soft costs, $1M financing, $2M developer fee and $0.5M reserves.

Most of buckets 2 through 5 lands in eligible basis. Land and reserves don't. See what counts in basis.

Key points
  • Acquisition, hard, soft, financing, fee, reserves.
  • Hard costs are the biggest bucket.
  • Not every bucket earns credits.
Sources
  • Example numbers are made up for illustration.

4.3 Why the bank lends so little

On a market-rate deal, the mortgage is often the biggest piece of the stack. On a tax credit deal, it's usually small. The reason is capped rents.

Lenders size a loan off net operating income (NOI) and a debt service coverage ratio (DSCR), commonly 1.15 to 1.25. Capped rents mean capped NOI, which means a capped loan.

Example, made-up numbers: 60 units at an average rent of $900 is $648,000 a year. Less 7% vacancy leaves $602,640. Less operating costs of $5,500 a unit ($330,000) leaves NOI of $272,640. At a 1.20 DSCR, the deal can carry $227,200 a year of debt service. At 6.5% over 35 years, that supports a loan of about $3.1 million, on a deal that might cost $20 million.

That's why the rest has to come from equity and soft money, and why higher interest rates hit affordable deals so hard.

Key points
  • Loans are sized off NOI and DSCR.
  • Capped rents cap the loan.
  • Often 15% to 25% of the stack.
  • Rate increases shrink it further.
Sources
  • Example numbers are made up; loan math is standard amortization at the rate and term shown.

4.4 The four most common soft-money sources

Soft money is the cheap or forgivable layer that closes the gap. The four you'll see most:

  • HOME: HUD block grants to states and cities, created in 1990, used for loans or grants to affordable housing.
  • CDBG: Community Development Block Grants, a broader HUD program cities sometimes use for site work or infrastructure.
  • National Housing Trust Fund: created in 2008 and funded by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, aimed mostly at extremely low-income households.
  • FHLB Affordable Housing Program (AHP): each Federal Home Loan Bank sets aside at least 10% of its prior year's net income for competitive grants, applied for through a member bank.

Plus state and local trust funds, and sometimes the housing authority. Every source brings its own rules: income targeting, affordability periods, and with federal money, extra requirements like accessibility (Section 504) and, in some cases, Davis-Bacon wages. Funding levels change every year.

Key points
  • HOME, CDBG, Housing Trust Fund, FHLB AHP.
  • Cheap or forgivable, but competitive and slow.
  • Federal grants can reduce eligible basis, so many are made as loans.
  • Each source adds its own rules.
Sources
  • HOME: Cranston-Gonzalez National Affordable Housing Act of 1990; 24 CFR Part 92.
  • National Housing Trust Fund: Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008; 24 CFR Part 93.
  • FHLB AHP: 12 U.S.C. § 1430(j); 12 CFR Part 1291.
  • CDBG: Housing and Community Development Act of 1974.

4.5 How the equity actually gets paid in

The investor doesn't write one check at closing. Equity comes in installments, each tied to a milestone that lowers the investor's risk:

  1. Closing.
  2. Construction completion.
  3. Placed in service and lease-up.
  4. Stabilization or conversion to the permanent loan.
  5. Final cost certification and IRS Form 8609s.
Example, made-up numbers: $8.3M of equity paid 20% at closing, 40% at completion, 30% at stabilization and 10% at 8609s.

Because most equity arrives after construction, a construction loan bridges the gap. Some investors pay more up front for a lower price; others pay later for a higher one. That timing is part of every pricing negotiation.

Key points
  • Equity comes in milestones, not one check.
  • A construction loan bridges the timing.
  • Up-front vs. later pay-in affects price.
  • The last piece often waits on the 8609s.
Sources
  • General industry practice; schedules are negotiated deal by deal.
  • IRS Form 8609, Low-Income Housing Credit Allocation and Certification.

4.6 The developer fee, and why part of it waits

The developer fee pays the developer for the time, risk and guarantees it takes to get a deal built. States cap it in the QAP, often around 15% of development costs, though the formula varies a lot by state.

Most of the fee is in eligible basis, so it earns credits. But it's rarely all paid in cash. Part of it is usually deferred: left in the deal as a source of funds and paid later out of cash flow.

Investors generally want the deferred piece to be realistically paid off within about 15 years. If it can't be, the IRS could question whether it belongs in basis, which would shrink the credits.

So a deferred fee is the developer betting its own pay on the property performing.

Key points
  • Capped by each state.
  • Mostly in eligible basis.
  • Part is deferred and paid from cash flow.
  • It has to be realistically payable.
Sources
  • State QAP developer fee limits (vary by state).
  • General tax practice on deferred developer fees; specifics depend on tax counsel.

4.7 Why construction costs sink deals

On a market-rate deal, higher costs can be passed on through higher rents. On a tax credit deal, they can't. Rents are capped by the income limits.

Higher costs do raise eligible basis, and so the credit amount. But states cap how much credit one deal can get, and many cap cost per unit. Once a deal hits those limits, every extra dollar of cost is a dollar of gap.

That gap has to come from somewhere: more soft money, more deferred fee, a value-engineered design, or fewer units. If none of those works, the deal dies, sometimes after the award.

A federal review found per-unit costs vary widely from state to state, and that states use very different cost controls.

Key points
  • Rents are capped, so costs can't be passed on.
  • Credit and cost caps limit how far basis helps.
  • Extra cost becomes gap.
  • Gaps kill deals, even after an award.
Sources
  • GAO-18-637, "Low-Income Housing Tax Credit: Improved Data and Oversight Would Strengthen Cost Assessment and Fraud Risk Management" (2018).
  • State QAP credit and cost limits (vary by state).
