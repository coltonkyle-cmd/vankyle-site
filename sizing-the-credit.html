2.1 Three numbers, one award

Every tax credit award comes from the same formula:

  • Eligible basis: what it cost to build the apartments. Land doesn't count.
  • Applicable fraction: the share of the building that's affordable, by units or by floor space, whichever is lower. If every unit is restricted, it's 100%.
  • Credit rate: at least 9% a year for a new 9% deal, at least 4% for a 4% deal.

Eligible basis × applicable fraction = qualified basis. Qualified basis × credit rate = the annual credit, claimed for 10 years.

Example, made-up numbers: $10M of eligible basis × 100% × 9% = $900,000 of credit a year, or $9 million over 10 years. The developer sells that to an investor for cash.

In practice, the state also checks the deal's funding gap and awards only what the deal needs, which can be less than the formula. More eligible basis still means more credit, which is why what counts as basis, and where you build, matter so much.

Key points
  • Eligible basis × applicable fraction × rate.
  • Land isn't in basis.
  • Claimed every year for 10 years.
  • States can award less than the formula if the deal needs less.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(a), § 42(b), § 42(c)(1), § 42(d)(1), § 42(f)(1).
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(m)(2) (credit limited to the amount needed for feasibility).

2.2 The 30% basis boost

Two identical buildings can get very different awards. The difference is the address.

In certain areas, the tax code lets a deal count 130% of its eligible basis:

  • Qualified Census Tracts (QCTs): tracts where at least half of households earn under 60% of area median income, or the poverty rate is 25% or more.
  • Difficult Development Areas (DDAs): places where land, construction and utility costs are high compared to local incomes.
  • State-designated buildings: a state agency can give the boost to a 9% building that needs it to be financially feasible. This one isn't available to bond-financed 4% deals.
Example, made-up numbers: $10M of basis becomes $13M. At 9%, that's $1.17M of credit a year instead of $900K, about $2.7M more over 10 years.

HUD redraws the QCT and DDA maps every year. The 2027 lists were announced in October 2026. A boost for all rural and tribal areas was proposed in 2025 but didn't make it into law.

Key points
  • Count 130% of eligible basis.
  • QCTs: lower-income tracts.
  • DDAs: high-cost areas.
  • Or the state designates the building (9% only).
  • The map changes every year.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(d)(5)(B).
  • HUD, 2027 Difficult Development Areas and Qualified Census Tracts (announced Oct. 5, 2026; Federal Register Oct. 6, 2026).
  • Rural basis boost omitted from P.L. 119-21: Housing Assistance Council; Moss Adams.

2.3 What counts in eligible basis

Since credits are sized off eligible basis, every line in the budget gets a question: is it in basis or not?

Usually in: construction of the residential building, much of the site work tied to it, architecture and engineering, construction-period interest and fees, permits, and the developer fee (within the state's cap). Common areas like a community room or laundry count if residents aren't charged extra to use them.

Usually out: land, permanent loan costs, reserves, marketing, syndication costs, and commercial space.

Things that shrink it: federal grants used for the building reduce eligible basis. That's why many federal soft funds are made as loans instead.

Existing buildings: for a rehab deal, the cost of buying an existing building can count as acquisition basis at the 4% rate, generally if it hasn't changed hands in the last 10 years. The rehab work itself has to meet a minimum: the greater of 20% of the building's basis or a per-unit amount ($9,000 per low-income unit for 2027).

Key points
  • Building costs and most soft costs are in.
  • Land, reserves and permanent loan costs are out.
  • Federal grants reduce basis.
  • Acquisition basis can earn 4% credits on existing buildings.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(d)(1)–(5), § 42(e)(3).
  • IRS Rev. Proc. 2026-35 (2027 rehab per-unit minimum).
  • Item-by-item treatment varies; deals rely on tax counsel and a cost certification.

2.4 From award to cash

Here's what a $1 million award actually turns into.

Example, made-up numbers: $1,000,000 of credit a year × 10 years = $10,000,000 of total credits. At 83 cents per dollar, the investor pays about $8,300,000 into the partnership.

A few things to know about that $8.3M:

  • It doesn't arrive all at once. Investors pay in installments tied to milestones. See the pay-in lesson.
  • The investor gets the credits plus the property's tax losses, mostly depreciation. Both count toward its return.
  • If the credits come in late or short, the agreement usually cuts the investor's payments. Those are called adjusters.
Key points
  • Annual credit × 10 × price = equity.
  • Paid in installments, not one check.
  • Investors get credits and tax losses.
  • Shortfalls trigger adjusters.
Sources
  • Example numbers and pricing are made up for illustration; actual pricing varies by deal and market.

2.5 The nonprofit set-aside

Federal law requires each state to reserve at least 10% of its credit budget for projects where a qualified nonprofit owns an interest and materially participates in development and operations.

A qualified nonprofit is a 501(c)(3) or 501(c)(4) whose exempt purposes include fostering low-income housing. It can't be controlled by a for-profit.

Many states go further, with their own nonprofit or community-based pools. That's one reason you see for-profit developers partner with local nonprofits and housing authorities.

Key points
  • At least 10% of every state's credits.
  • The nonprofit must materially participate.
  • States can set aside more.
  • It's a big reason for nonprofit partnerships.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(h)(5).

2.6 Your state's 2027 credit budget

Each state's annual 9% credit budget is set by a per-person formula, adjusted for inflation every year.

For 2027: the greater of $3.53 per resident or $4,082,400 for small states. That's up from $3.416 in 2026, the first year with the permanent 12% increase.

Example, made-up numbers: a state with 5 million residents gets about $17.65 million of annual credit authority. Since each award is claimed for 10 years, that's about $176.5 million of total credits handed out in one year.

On top of the per-person amount, states can re-award credits that were returned by earlier deals and may get a share of a small national pool of unused credits.

Two things to remember: the budget is in annual credits, and it's almost always oversubscribed. States commonly get several dollars of requests for every dollar they can award.

Key points
  • 2027: $3.53 per resident, or $4,082,400 minimum.
  • Budgets are in annual credits.
  • Returned credits get re-awarded.
  • Demand far exceeds supply.
Sources
  • IRS Rev. Proc. 2026-35 (2027 amounts); Novogradac, 2026.
  • IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 (2026 amount of $3.416).
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(h)(3).

2.7 The cap behind every 4% deal

4% deals don't use the state's credit budget, but they're still rationed. The bonds they need count against the state's private activity bond volume cap.

For 2027: each state's cap is the greater of $135 per resident or $410,520,000.

Housing isn't the only use. The same cap covers single-family mortgage revenue bonds, some industrial and student loan bonds, and other uses. The state decides how to split it, and in many states housing applications far exceed the cap.

That's why the 2026 change to a 25% bond test matters. A project that used to need bonds for half its cost now needs them for a quarter, so the same cap can support more deals.

Key points
  • 4% deals need volume-cap bonds.
  • 2027: $135 per resident, or $410.52M minimum.
  • Housing competes with other uses.
  • The 25% test stretches the cap further.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 146 (volume cap); § 42(h)(4).
  • IRS Rev. Proc. 2026-35 (2027 amounts).
  • Public Law 119-21 (25% test for bonds issued after 2025).
