LIHTC in five minutes

New to affordable housing? These are the basics I wish someone had handed me on day one. When you want more, the full course goes deeper.

1. What LIHTC is

The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit is a federal tax credit, written into Section 42 of the tax code. Each state gets a pot of credits every year and awards them to apartment projects through a competition run by its housing finance agency.

The developer doesn't keep the credits. An investor, usually a bank, puts cash into the project in exchange for them. That cash is equity, and it's what lets the building get built with less debt, which is how the rents stay low.

Example, made-up numbers: a project receives $1,000,000 of credits a year for 10 years, or $10 million total. If an investor pays 85 cents per dollar of credit, that's $8.5 million of equity for the building.

2. It's not public housing

Most LIHTC properties are privately owned and privately managed. Public housing is owned by a housing authority. They can overlap, like when public housing is rebuilt using tax credits, but they're different programs.

3. How long it stays affordable

There's a 15-year compliance period, and then an extended use period of at least another 15 years. So a typical deal stays affordable for at least 30 years, and many states require longer.

4. Who can live there, and how rent is set

Every deal picks one of three minimum set-asides:

  • 20/50: at least 20% of units for households earning 50% or less of area median income (AMI).
  • 40/60: at least 40% of units for households at 60% of AMI or less.
  • Income averaging: the restricted units average 60% of AMI or less, and no unit goes above 80%.

Rent is capped too. Gross rent, including a utility allowance, can't be more than 30% of the income limit for that unit size. That's why tax credit rents are set by the income limits, not the market.

5. 9% vs 4% deals

There are two flavors. The names come from roughly how much of a building's eligible cost the credits are worth each year.

  • 9% credits are worth about 70% of eligible costs in present value. They're scarce, so states award them competitively.
  • 4% credits are worth about 30%. They come with tax-exempt bond financing instead of the competition, so they're easier to get but need more money from other sources.

Either way, the credits are claimed over 10 years.

6. What changed in 2026

A 2025 federal tax law made two big changes that started in 2026:

  • States' 9% credit allocations went up 12%, permanently.
  • The bond test for 4% deals dropped from 50% to 25%, meaning a project needs fewer tax-exempt bonds to get 4% credits. That frees up bond capacity for more deals.
