6.1 You won. The clock started.

Winning tax credits feels like the finish line. It's really the starting gun.

  • Before the award: the developer pays for a market study.
  • Within 12 months: if the building won't be finished in the award year, more than 10% of its expected basis has to be spent within a year of the allocation.
  • By the end of year 2: the building has to be placed in service by the end of the second calendar year after the award year.
Example, made-up numbers: award in June 2027. Spend more than 10% by June 2028. Placed in service by December 31, 2029.

Miss a deadline and the allocation fails; the credits go back to the state to hand out again. Closing the land, the loans and the investor, permits, and construction all have to fit inside that window.

Key points
  • Market study before the award.
  • 10% of basis spent within 12 months.
  • Placed in service by the end of year 2.
  • Miss it and the credits go back.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(m)(1)(A)(iii), § 42(h)(1)(E), § 42(h)(3)(C).
  • Example dates are made up.

6.2 When a property breaks the rules

Tax credit properties report to the state every year and get monitored, with file reviews of tenant income records and physical inspections.

When the state finds a problem, like an over-income tenant, an unsafe unit or rent over the cap, it tells the owner, who generally gets up to 90 days to fix it, extendable to six months. The state must then report it to the IRS on Form 8823, even if it was corrected.

Consequences during the 15-year compliance period:

  • Credits can be lost for any unit and year out of compliance.
  • If the building's qualified basis drops, part of the credits already claimed can be recaptured, with interest.

After year 15, the IRS steps back, and the state enforces the extended use agreement.

Key points
  • Annual reporting and state monitoring.
  • A correction period, then Form 8823 to the IRS.
  • Lost credits and recapture during years 1–15.
  • The state enforces years 16–30+.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(j), § 42(m)(1)(B)(iii).
  • Treas. Reg. § 1.42-5.
  • IRS, Guide for Completing Form 8823.

6.3 Year 15: how the investor exits

Once the 15-year compliance period ends, the investor has gotten its credits and usually wants out. Common paths:

  • Sale to the general partner: the developer side buys the investor's interest or the property, often under an option written into the partnership agreement.
  • Nonprofit right of first refusal: a qualified nonprofit, tenant group or government agency can buy at a minimum price, roughly the outstanding debt plus the investor's exit taxes.
  • Qualified contract: after year 14, an owner can ask the state to find a buyer at a formula price. If none is found within a year, the extended use period ends, though existing tenants keep protections for three more years. Many states now require owners to waive this option.

Year 15 is also when many properties get rehabbed with a new round of credits, called resyndication.

Key points
  • Investors usually exit after year 15.
  • The GP often buys them out.
  • Nonprofits can have a right of first refusal.
  • Many states require waiving the qualified contract.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(i)(7) (right of first refusal), § 42(h)(6)(E)–(F), (I) (qualified contract).
  • Not legal advice; exits depend on each partnership agreement.

6.4 How old public housing gets rebuilt (RAD)

Public housing nationwide has a big backlog of repairs, and the old funding model doesn't let housing authorities borrow against it. The Rental Assistance Demonstration (RAD), created in 2012, fixes that.

RAD converts public housing units to long-term Section 8 contracts, either project-based vouchers or project-based rental assistance. With a steady contract, the property can take on debt and tax credit equity to renovate or rebuild.

Residents keep key protections, including the right to return after construction without being re-screened.

Scale: Congress capped conversions at 455,000 public housing units, and HUD says about half of the public housing that existed in 2012 has now converted. Some deals blend RAD with Section 18, another tool for repositioning public housing. In May 2026, HUD began winding down Restore-Rebuild (formerly Faircloth-to-RAD), which had let authorities use RAD to build new units.

Key points
  • Converts public housing to Section 8 contracts.
  • Lets housing authorities borrow and use tax credits.
  • Residents have a right to return.
  • Capped at 455,000 units.
Sources
  • HUD, RAD Overview (August 2026).
  • National Housing Law Project, RAD resources (455,000-unit cap).
  • Nelson Mullins and Nixon Peabody alerts, May 2026 (Restore-Rebuild wind-down).
