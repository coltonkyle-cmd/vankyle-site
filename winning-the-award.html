3.1 What every state has to reward

Every state writes its own scoring plan, the Qualified Allocation Plan (QAP). But federal law sets the floor. Every QAP must give preference to:

  • Projects serving the lowest-income residents.
  • Projects that stay affordable the longest.
  • Projects in qualified census tracts that contribute to a local community revitalization plan.

It also has to weigh a required list of criteria: location, housing needs, the project and sponsor, tenant populations with special needs, public housing waitlists, families with children, energy efficiency, and historic buildings.

And it has to keep watching after the award, with compliance monitoring, site visits and reports to the IRS.

Everything else, the points, weights, set-asides and tiebreakers, is up to the state. That's why every QAP reads so differently, and why developers re-read them every year.

Key points
  • Three required preferences.
  • A required list of criteria to weigh.
  • Ongoing monitoring.
  • Points and tiebreakers are up to each state.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(m)(1)(B)–(C).

3.2 The team it takes to build one deal

One tax credit deal takes a lot of people. The core seats:

  • Developer: finds the site, runs the application, guarantees the deal.
  • Architect and engineers: design to code, to the QAP and to accessibility rules.
  • General contractor: prices and builds it.
  • Syndicator or investor: buys the credits.
  • Lenders: a construction loan and a permanent loan, sometimes the same bank.
  • Attorneys: usually several, for the developer, investor, lender and bonds.
  • Market analyst: proves there's demand.
  • Property manager: leases it up and keeps it in compliance for decades.

Add a CPA for the cost certification, often a tax credit consultant, and on public housing deals, the housing authority. Every one of them has to say yes before closing.

Key points
  • At least eight core seats.
  • Several attorneys, not one.
  • The property manager matters for 30+ years.
  • Everyone has to sign off before closing.
Sources
  • General industry practice; roles vary by deal.

3.3 The market study

Before a state can award credits, federal law requires a comprehensive market study of the housing needs of low-income people in the area. It's done by a disinterested third party approved by the state, and the developer pays for it.

The study answers one question: will people rent these apartments at these rents? Things it looks at:

  • Capture rate: the share of qualified, income-eligible renters the project needs to fill. Lower is safer.
  • Absorption: how many months to lease up.
  • Rent advantage: how far the capped rents sit below comparable market rents.
  • Competition: other affordable properties nearby, open or in the pipeline.

States set their own thresholds, and many follow national market study standards. A weak study can sink an application before scoring even starts.

Key points
  • Required by federal law before an award.
  • Third party, paid for by the developer.
  • Capture rate, absorption and rent advantage.
  • States set the pass/fail lines.
Sources
  • 26 U.S.C. § 42(m)(1)(A)(iii).
  • Metric definitions vary by state; see each state's QAP and market study guide.

3.4 How to read an award list

Every state posts who applied and who won. It's public, and it's one of the best ways to learn the business.

  1. Find the list. Search your state housing finance agency's site for "housing tax credit awards" or "application log."
  2. Check the set-aside or region. Most states award by region and by pool (nonprofit, rural, elderly, at-risk). Applications only compete inside their pool.
  3. Look at the scores. Winners are often separated by a point or two, and ties are broken by rules written in the QAP.
  4. Divide credits by units. Annual credits per unit shows how much each deal needed, and how far over the state's limits it was pushing.
  5. Watch the waitlist. Returned credits go to the next deal in line, sometimes months later.
Key points
  • Award lists are public.
  • Deals compete within regions and pools.
  • Scores are often one or two points apart.
  • Credits per unit is the quickest comparison.
Sources
  • State housing finance agency award and application logs; formats vary by state.
